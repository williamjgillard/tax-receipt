“All of these projects had been approved in the past … all of them were stalled for want of financing, until today.”
Needs contextTrue as far as it goes — but it also means the $2.7B isn't creating projects from scratch, just financing ones already stuck in the pipeline.
Carney made this remark defending his government's $2.7-billion, three-year commitment (with the City of Toronto) to 18 housing projects delivering more than 5,600 rental homes, 1,800 of them deeply affordable or rent-controlled, with construction starting on roughly 4,500 of those units by the end of 2026. The money flows through two real federal financing streams — Build Canada Homes and CMHC's Apartment Construction Loan Program — and Carney's claim that the projects were previously approved but stalled for lack of financing is accurate; that's also, functionally, the basis of Conservative housing critic Scott Aitchison's dismissal of the event as "just another repackaging of projects already announced or already under construction." Both things are true at once: this is real federal financing turning stalled, previously-approved projects into actual construction (not nothing), but it is not the origination of 5,600 brand-new proposals the "new rental homes" framing implies to a casual reader. The dollar figures and unit counts check out against the government's own release and Toronto's; the gap is between "new homes" and "new financing for existing plans."
Source: Prime Minister of Canada, news release, August 5, 2026; CBC News, August 5, 2026

Speaking outside a Rio Tinto aluminum facility in Quebec, Carney argued that Trump's 50% tariff on aluminum imports (imposed June 2025) is landing mostly on American buyers rather than being driven by global price moves, citing a roughly 52% year-over-year rise in the U.S. aluminum producer price index. Independent U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data backs the shape of this: the PPI for alumina and aluminum production and processing was up 48.75% year-over-year as of May 2026, and the Associated General Contractors of America has separately found that U.S. sellers are pushing tariff costs straight through to buyers, with no evidence of domestic absorption. Both the figure and the underlying pass-through mechanism hold up against sources outside Carney's own office.

“Canada has the highest food inflation in the G7.”
Needs contextThe number is right. Who gets blamed for it isn't established by that number.
The comparative statistic checks out against OECD figures. Where the claim overreaches is the unstated next step — pinning it on one government. The Bank of Canada and the Competition Bureau have both pointed to international energy shocks, global supply-chain disruption, and trade uncertainty as drivers of food prices — forces that predate and extend past any single administration. True number, unsupported causal story attached to it.

“[The condo-purchase program is] one of the fastest and most efficient ways to increase housing supply.”
MisleadingBuying existing units isn't the same as adding new ones.
The government's plan to buy 2,200 completed condo units in Metro Vancouver reallocates existing inventory to affordable housing — a legitimate goal, but not the same thing as expanding supply. CMHC data showed 4,376 completed condos already sitting vacant in Metro Vancouver at the time, and multiple housing analysts and brokers said the purchase wouldn't trigger any new construction. Purchase prices and selection criteria also weren't disclosed, making the "efficient" half of the claim unverifiable on its own terms.

Widely circulated, 2026 immigration debateReported December 2025
“Canada's population fell for the first time since Confederation.”
TrueConfirmed directly by Statistics Canada.
Statistics Canada confirmed the country's population shrank by roughly 100,000–103,500 people over 2025 — the first calendar-year decline in records dating back to the 1940s, and the first on record since Confederation by StatCan's own account. The drop was driven mainly by departing temporary residents: international students and temporary foreign workers leaving faster than new arrivals replaced them.
Source: Statistics Canada, population estimates, released December 2025

“The 2026 plan cuts temporary resident admissions and brings temporary residents below 5% of the population by the end of 2027.”
TrueMatches the government's own published targets exactly.
The government's own 2026–2028 Immigration Levels Plan sets new temporary resident admissions at 385,000 in 2026 and 370,000 in 2027 and 2028, including a 50% cut to new international study permits, with an explicitly stated objective of bringing the temporary resident share of Canada's population under 5% by the end of 2027. This is a case of the government's stated plan matching what the government said its plan does — the open question is execution, not the claim itself.
Source: IRCC, Supplementary Information for the 2026–2028 Immigration Levels Plan
