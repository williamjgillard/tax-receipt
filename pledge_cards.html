Government of Canada / AlbertaCommitted November 2025
Designate a new oil pipeline from Alberta to the West Coast as a “project of national interest” under the Building Canada Act, fast-tracking its federal approvals.
  • November 2025Canada and Alberta sign an energy pact in which Ottawa commits to eventually declaring a west-coast pipeline a project in the national interest.
  • October 1, 2026In Fort McMurray alongside Alberta Premier Danielle Smith, Prime Minister Carney designates a proposed ~1,200-km pipeline from Bruderheim, Alta. to Delta, B.C. — now named Pacific Link — as the first-ever project of national interest listed under the Building Canada Act, on the recommendation of Minister Dominic LeBlanc.
  • October 7, 2026Cold Lake First Nation files for judicial review in Federal Court, seeking to quash the national interest designation and have the court declare that Ottawa owes it a duty to consult; the band says it was not meaningfully consulted on Pacific Link or the related Pathways carbon-capture project. It is the first legal challenge to the designation; the Union of BC Indian Chiefs and the Coldwater Indian Band have also publicly opposed it without yet filing their own court applications.
  • October 30, 2026WẄJOȚẄẄP (Tsartlip First Nation) files its own application for judicial review in Federal Court, becoming the second First Nation to challenge the national-interest designation; its case invokes the 1852 North Saanich Treaty's protection of its members' fishing rights and the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, and asks the court to require Ottawa to meaningfully consult it before any national-interest decision stands. Other B.C. communities along the pipeline and tanker routes, plus Fraser River salmon-dependent nations further away, have also raised objections, though not all have filed their own court applications; no ruling has been issued on either challenge yet.
  • November 2–3, 2026A wider wave of litigation reaches Federal Court: several more B.C. First Nations and bands — including Boston Bar, Cheam, Lower Nicola, Kwantlen, Kwikwetlem, and Skeetchestn — file their own separate applications for judicial review, and the St’at’imc Chiefs Council (representing nine nations in B.C.’s Interior and Fraser Valley) launches a further legal action, each citing inadequate consultation and the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples. Combined with Cold Lake's and Tsartlip's earlier filings, reporting now puts the number of First Nations challenging the designation in Federal Court at nine to eleven depending on the count; no hearing date or ruling has been set on any of the applications.
  • No date setThe designation streamlines and consolidates federal permitting but does not itself approve construction; the proponent still needs to clear regulatory review, Indigenous consultation, and financing before building could start. Government targets permissions to begin design and construction as early as September 2027, with public hearings and Indigenous forums expected over winter/spring 2027; financing and the final route remain unresolved, and most Indigenous communities consulted so far say they aren't prepared to support the designation.

Government of Canada / Prime Minister Mark CarneyCommitted November 4, 2025
Balance the federal government's day-to-day operating budget by fiscal 2028–29.
  • November 4, 2025Budget 2025 sets two new fiscal anchors: balancing the operating budget (day-to-day spending, excluding capital investment) by 2028–29, and a declining deficit-to-GDP ratio.
  • September 15, 2026At the Canada Investment Summit, Carney announces Ottawa is now on track to balance the operating budget a year early, in fiscal 2027–28, citing a 10% year-over-year jump in federal revenues.
  • September 24–25, 2026The Parliamentary Budget Officer publishes an analysis disputing that timeline, projecting the operating budget won't actually balance until 2029–30 — a year later than the original target and two years behind Carney's claim — and flagging that the government's operating-vs-capital spending classifications are inconsistent.
  • November 5, 2026Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne tables Budget 2026, projecting a $62.3-billion federal deficit for fiscal 2026–27 and reaffirming the government's claim that the operating budget will balance in fiscal 2027–28 — one year ahead of the original 2028–29 anchor. The budget does not engage with the Parliamentary Budget Officer's competing 2029–30 projection, and sets no timeline for eliminating the overall, capital-inclusive deficit.
  • Target: 2027–28 (government) / 2029–30 (PBO)Budget 2026 leaves the dispute unresolved — Ottawa maintains 2027–28, the PBO maintains 2029–30 — and which projection is correct won't be known until actual fiscal year-end results are reported.

Government of CanadaCommitted September 21, 2026
Compress major federal project reviews to “one project, one decision, in one year” via the Building Canada Strong Act.
  • September 21, 2026Bill C-39, the Building Canada Strong Act, receives first reading — a coordinated federal review process meant to compress a historically five-year approvals timeline (assessments, permits, and consultations run concurrently) into a single federal decision within a legislated one-year window, alongside changes to federal labour relations aimed at reducing transportation-sector strikes.
  • September 23–25, 2026Bill C-39 is debated at second reading in the House of Commons; the government moves a motion to refer it to committee, but the House has not yet voted on that motion or begun committee study.
  • Early October 2026Bill C-39 passes second reading 296–26, with Conservative MPs voting alongside the government while the Bloc Québécois, NDP, and Greens oppose it. The bill is referred to a special Legislative Committee on Bill C-39, which votes on October 9, 2026 to begin clause-by-clause review in mid-November and invites Transport Minister Steven MacKinnon, Environment Minister Julie Dabrusin, Jobs Minister Patty Hajdu, and Canada-U.S. Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc to testify.
  • October 19, 2026Federal ministers, including Transport Minister Steven MacKinnon and Canada-U.S. Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc, appear before the special committee to defend the bill, as labour, environmental, and Indigenous groups continue to raise concerns about its pace; LeBlanc tells the committee “a more efficient process does not mean a less rigorous one” on the duty to consult. The committee's October 26 deadline for public stakeholder briefs has since passed.
  • No date setClause-by-clause committee review was scheduled to begin mid-November 2026; this update could not confirm from available reporting whether that session has actually started. Royal assent and the first project actually reviewed under the new one-year standard haven't happened yet. (Note: a separate, already-law fast-track mechanism under the earlier Building Canada Act used its own national-interest designation for the first time on October 1, 2026 — see the Pacific Link pipeline card above — but that is not a review conducted under this bill's new one-year standard.)

Prime Minister Mark CarneyCommitted June 9, 2025
Hit NATO's 2%-of-GDP defence spending target — five years ahead of the prior 2032 goal.
  • June 9, 2025Carney announces Canada will meet the 2% target within the 2025–26 fiscal year, roughly $9B in new spending that year.
  • June 2025, NATO Hague SummitCanada joins NATO allies' new pledge to invest 5% of GDP annually by 2035 (3.5% core defence + 1.5% broader security).
  • March 26, 2026Carney announces Canada achieved the 2% target for fiscal 2025–26, citing over $63B spent that year — the largest year-over-year defence increase in generations.
  • November 5, 2026Budget 2026 sketches a path toward the 2035 goal but adds only $755 million in net new defence funding over five years (including $2.3 billion for a Defence Drone Initiative within that envelope); it projects core defence spending reaching $99.2 billion (2.6% of GDP) by 2030–31, with total security-related spending reaching 4.1% of GDP that year — still well short of the 3.5% core / 5% total goal for 2035, and without a full spending breakdown for the years beyond 2030.
  • Target: 2035Budget 2026 does not close the gap to the 3.5%-of-GDP core target; the Parliamentary Budget Officer estimates reaching it will require roughly $33.5B/year in additional defence spending on average through 2035 (projecting about $63B added to the 2035–36 deficit), and C.D. Howe Institute says spending will need to nearly triple by 2035 to meet it — both still describing the post-2030 financing plan as unresolved.

  • February 2026~100 days in, the agency reports agreements representing over 7,500 homes across Ottawa, Winnipeg, and Nunavut.

Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship CanadaTabled November 4, 2025
2026–2028 Immigration Levels Plan — cut temporary resident admissions, hold permanent resident admissions near current levels.
  • November 4, 2025Plan tabled: permanent resident admissions set at 380,000/year through 2028; new temporary resident arrivals targeted at 385,000 in 2026, then 370,000 in 2027–28 (down from 367,750 work permits and 305,900 study permits in 2025); explicit goal of temporary residents under 5% of the population by end of 2027.
  • April 30, 2026IRCC tells a parliamentary committee that the non-permanent resident share of Canada's population has fallen to 6.5%, down from a peak of 7.6% — real progress, but still well above the under-5%-by-2027 target.
  • November 5, 2026The successor 2027–2029 Immigration Levels Plan is released alongside Budget 2026 — after the November 1 statutory deadline had already passed without a published plan — holding permanent resident admissions at 380,000 a year and new temporary resident arrivals at 370,000 a year through 2029, essentially unchanged from the prior plan's later years. IRCC describes the 2028 and 2029 figures as notional, to be confirmed or adjusted annually.
  • No date set2026's actual admission numbers, and progress toward the under-5%-of-population target for temporary residents by end of 2027, won't be confirmed against the plan's targets until IRCC publishes year-end figures.
Sources: IRCC, Supplementary Information for the 2026–2028 Immigration Levels Plan; IRCC, SECU committee briefing, Immigration Levels Plan, April 30, 2026; BNN Bloomberg, “Ottawa to shift more temporary residents to permanent status as broad levels flatten,” November 5, 2026; BNN Bloomberg, “Canada's new immigration plan revealed in federal budget,” November 6, 2026.
